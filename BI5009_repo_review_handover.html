D. Moving from threshold-based testing to estimation and intervals

This is the change Alex has wanted to make for some time. The aim is not to remove hypothesis testing. Students will meet p values everywhere in the literature and need to read them fluently. The aim is to demote the threshold from the conclusion to one piece of evidence, and to promote the parameter estimate and its interval to the headline.

D1. The specific error that must go

linear_model_3 Q8 contains three instances of accepting the null:

As the P value (0.118) is > 0.05 we fail to reject this null hypothesis and conclude that the intercepts for graze level 1 and graze level 2 are the same.

As the P value (0.082) > 0.05 we conclude that the slopes are not different (i.e. they are the same).

The first clause of each is correct. The conversion to a positive claim of equality is not licensed by any test. A p value is computed conditional on the null being true; it cannot be inverted into a statement about whether the null is true. A large p value has two indistinguishable causes: the effect is negligible, or the study cannot resolve it.

These data cannot resolve it. The FGRAZE2 contrast is −6.06 with a 95% interval of −13.72 to +1.60 birds. Bird abundance in these data runs 1.5 to 39.6 with sd 10.1, and the raw difference in group means between graze 1 and 2 is 9.2 birds. So the interval is compatible with graze level 2 having nearly fourteen fewer birds at a one-hectare patch, a larger effect than the raw group difference and about 1.4 sd of the response. It is simultaneously compatible with a small increase.

The interaction case is starker. FGRAZE2:LOGAREA has an interval of −0.56 to 9.10, while graze level 1’s own slope is 4.14. The solution calls the slopes “the same” when the data are consistent with graze level 2’s slope being more than twice graze level 1’s. With eleven sites in graze level 2 that is a precision problem, not a null result.

Why it matters disproportionately here: these are almost the only non-significant coefficients students meet in the whole four-exercise sequence. Every other contrast gets written up as a difference. So this passage is the students’ entire worked template for reporting a result that does not cross the threshold, and Assessment 3 is where they will reuse it.

D2. Principles to apply throughout

  1. Estimate first, uncertainty second, test third. Every interpretation should lead with the estimate in the units of the response, follow with the interval, and mention the p value last if at all.
  2. Never assert equality from a large p value. Standard replacement phrasing: “we have no clear evidence that X differs from Y”, followed by what the interval does not rule out.
  3. Keep the threshold visible but framed. Students must recognise “significant at p < 0.05” in papers. Add a short standing note explaining that 0.05 is a convention, not a property of nature, and that the classification it produces is not the finding.
  4. Interpret on the response scale. “9.78 more birds per tenfold increase in patch area” beats “the slope is significant”.
  5. Say when the answer is “we cannot tell”. This is a legitimate and publishable result, and students should see it written up as one.

D4. A standing note to add to the course site

Suggested placement: a short block in exercises.Rmd before the Section 2 list, and referenced from linear_model_1 Q5.

A note on p values and confidence intervals.

Throughout these exercises you will see p values, and you will see them everywhere in the published literature, so it is important that you can read them. A p value answers one narrow question: if there were genuinely no effect, how often would we see data at least this extreme? It does not tell you the probability that there is no effect, and it does not tell you how big an effect is.

The 0.05 threshold is a convention, not a fact about nature. A result at p = 0.049 and one at p = 0.051 are practically identical, and treating them as different kinds of finding is a mistake you will nevertheless see made regularly in print.

Two consequences we will hold to throughout this course:

  1. Lead with the estimate and its confidence interval, not the test. The interval tells you the size of the effect and how precisely you have measured it, which is what a reader actually needs.
  2. A large p value never means “no difference”. It means you have not detected one, which may be because there is none, or because your study is too small or too noisy to see it. Look at the interval: if it stretches from “trivial” to “enormous”, the honest conclusion is that you cannot tell.

You will meet the threshold-based style constantly when reading papers. Learn to read it, and to translate it into estimates and intervals in your own head.

E1. linear_model_1: new Q14 (after the optional Q12 and Q13)

14. Time to write this up. Imagine this analysis is going into the results section of your thesis. Using your model output from Questions 5, 7 and 12, complete the following paragraph. I have given you the skeleton; you supply the numbers and the missing words.

Bird abundance increased with forest patch area. A linear model of abundance against log10-transformed patch area estimated an increase of ______ birds (95% CI: ______ to ______) for every tenfold increase in patch area (F, = ______, p ______). The model explained ______% of the variation in bird abundance (n = ______ patches).

Now answer three short questions about what you have written:

  1. Why is the effect expressed per tenfold increase rather than per unit of LOGAREA? Which is more useful to a reader, and why?

  2. Your interval has a lower and an upper end. Write one sentence describing the smallest effect these data are reasonably consistent with, and one describing the largest. Would your ecological conclusion change between those two?

  3. The paragraph above reports a p value but does not use the word “significant”. Rewrite the first sentence so that it does use that word, then say which version you find more informative and why.

Model answer for the solutions file

(preceded by the standing caveat above)

Bird abundance increased with forest patch area. A linear model of abundance against log10-transformed patch area estimated an increase of 9.78 birds (95% CI: 7.76 to 11.81) for every tenfold increase in patch area (F1,65 = 92.85, p < 0.001). The model explained 58.8% of the variation in bird abundance (n = 67 patches).

  1. Because the model is fitted on the log10 scale, a one-unit change in LOGAREA is a tenfold change in area. Saying “9.78 more birds per tenfold increase in area” is directly interpretable; “9.78 birds per unit LOGAREA” requires the reader to do the back-transformation themselves. If a tenfold step is too coarse for your system, quote a doubling instead: log10(2) = 0.301, so a doubling of patch area is associated with 0.301 × 9.78 ≈ 2.9 more birds.

  2. Smallest: these data are consistent with an increase of about 7.8 birds per tenfold increase in area. Largest: about 11.8 birds. Both ends describe a substantial effect (bird abundance in this dataset ranges from 1.5 to 39.6), so the ecological conclusion, that patch area matters and matters a lot, holds across the whole interval. This is a well-resolved result. Contrast it with the interval you will meet in linear_model_3 Q8, where the two ends imply quite different conclusions.

  3. With the word: “Bird abundance increased significantly with forest patch area (p < 0.001).” That sentence is shorter, and it is what you will most often see in print, but it has thrown away everything a reader needs: it does not say by how much, in what units, or how precisely. “Significant” here means only “detectable”, not “large” or “important”. The first version tells a reader that patch area is worth managing; the second only tells them that we looked.

A note on n. Always give it. Two studies reporting the same estimate with n = 67 and n = 6700 are not making the same claim, and the interval is what reveals the difference.

E2. linear_model_2: new Q12 (after the optional Q11)

12. Again, imagine writing this up for your thesis. This time I am not going to give you the sentences, only the ingredients. Write a short results paragraph (four or five sentences is plenty) reporting the effect of grazing intensity on bird abundance. Your paragraph must contain:

  • the overall test of whether abundance differs among grazing levels, with its F statistic and degrees of freedom
  • the estimated mean abundance at the lowest grazing intensity
  • the size of the difference between the lowest and highest grazing intensities, with a confidence interval
  • a statement about the intermediate levels (2, 3 and 4) that does not require you to list all four contrasts one by one
  • the sample size

Two things to think about while you write:

  1. Your summary() table gives you four contrasts, all against graze level 1. Reporting all four in prose is tedious to read and hides the pattern. What is the pattern here, and how could you convey it in one sentence? (Hint: look at the group means from Q4 and the plot from Q11.)

  2. Would a table or a figure serve a reader better than prose for this result? If so, what would you put in it, and what would you keep in the text?

Model answer for the solutions file

(preceded by the standing caveat)

Mean bird abundance differed among the five grazing intensity levels (F4,62 = 14.98, p < 0.001; n = 67 patches). Abundance was highest at the lowest grazing intensity (mean 28.6 birds, 95% CI: 24.5 to 32.8) and lowest at the highest intensity (mean 6.3 birds), a difference of 22.3 birds (95% CI: 16.5 to 28.2). Grazing levels 2, 3 and 4 were similar to one another (means 19.4, 20.2 and 19.0 birds) and all lay roughly 8 to 10 birds below level 1. The pattern is therefore not a smooth decline across the grazing gradient: most of the effect is concentrated at the highest grazing intensity. Grazing intensity accounted for 49.2% of the variation in bird abundance.

  1. The pattern is a step, not a gradient. Levels 2, 3 and 4 are effectively interchangeable, level 1 is higher and level 5 is much lower. Saying that in one sentence is far more useful to a reader than four contrast statements, and it is the kind of structure that would inform a management recommendation. Note that “similar to one another” is a description of the estimated means, not a claim that they are identical; we have not tested them against each other and should not imply that we have.

  2. A figure. This result is a set of five group means with uncertainty, which is exactly what the plot in Q11 shows, and a reader takes the pattern from it in two seconds. Plot the five estimated means with their 95% intervals, and keep in the text only the overall test, the largest contrast and the sample size. Avoid a bar chart with error bars; the boxplot from Q4 or a plot of means with intervals both show the data better.

A caution about the intercept. The summary() table tests whether the intercept differs from zero (p < 2e-16). That is the hypothesis “there are no birds at all in ungrazed patches”, which nobody believes and nobody needed testing. Do not report it. A statistically impeccable result can still be biologically uninteresting, and part of writing up well is deciding what to leave out.

E3. linear_model_3: new Q10 (after the fitted-lines plot in Q9)

10. Write up this analysis for your thesis. No sentence frames this time, and no ingredient list: you decide what belongs. Aim for one paragraph plus a reference to your figure from Q9.

Before you start, three things to get right, because they are the three that people most often get wrong when reporting an interaction:

  1. You have a significant interaction. What does that mean for how you report the main effects of LOGAREA and FGRAZE? Is “bird abundance increases with patch area” a statement you can make on its own here?

  2. Look at the FGRAZE2 contrast (estimate −6.06, 95% CI −13.7 to +1.6) and the FGRAZE2:LOGAREA contrast (estimate 4.27, 95% CI −0.56 to 9.10). Neither interval excludes zero. Write one sentence reporting each of them without claiming that the two grazing levels are the same. Then write one further sentence explaining what the width of those intervals tells you about what this study could and could not resolve.

  3. Your model has 10 parameters and 57 residual degrees of freedom. Does reporting all 10 estimates serve a reader? If not, what would you report instead, and where would the full table go?

Model answer for the solutions file

(preceded by the standing caveat)

The relationship between bird abundance and forest patch area depended on grazing intensity (interaction F4,57 = 4.09, p = 0.006; model R² = 0.80, n = 67). Abundance increased with log10 patch area at every grazing level, but the rate of increase varied roughly fourfold, from 4.1 birds per tenfold increase in area at the lowest grazing intensity to 17.8 at level 4 (Figure X). Because the effect of patch area differs among grazing levels, no single estimate of the area effect describes these data, and the main effects should not be interpreted in isolation. Patch area therefore matters most in moderately grazed patches and least in lightly grazed ones, where abundance is already high in small patches.

  1. With a significant interaction, “bird abundance increases with patch area” is not a complete statement, because the size of that increase is conditional on grazing level. The LOGAREA coefficient in the table is not “the effect of area”; it is the effect of area at graze level 1 only. Similarly each FGRAZE coefficient is a difference in intercepts at LOGAREA = 0, which is a one-hectare patch. Report the interaction first, then describe the pattern of conditional effects, and only ever quote a main effect with its condition attached.

  2. FGRAZE2: at a one-hectare patch, abundance at grazing level 2 was estimated to be 6.1 birds lower than at level 1 (95% CI: 13.7 lower to 1.6 higher). FGRAZE2:LOGAREA: the increase in abundance per tenfold increase in area was estimated to be 4.3 birds greater at grazing level 2 than at level 1 (95% CI: 0.6 lower to 9.1 greater).

    What the widths tell you: both intervals are wide relative to the effects being estimated. The slope interval spans a difference of nearly 9 birds, which is more than twice the graze level 1 slope of 4.14, so these data are consistent with the two levels behaving almost identically and with level 2 responding far more steeply to patch area. With only 11 sites at grazing level 2, this study could not resolve the comparison. The correct conclusion is “we cannot tell”, and that is a legitimate thing to write. It is not the same as “there is no difference”, and writing it as though it were would be a misreport.

  3. No. Ten coefficients in prose is unreadable, and most of them are contrasts against an arbitrary baseline that no reader cares about. Report the interaction test, the range of fitted slopes, the figure, and the model R² and n. Put the full coefficient table in an appendix or supplementary material if the journal wants it. The figure from Q9 does more work than the table: it shows five fitted lines whose slopes visibly differ, which is the finding.

One more thing worth saying explicitly. The three grazing levels whose intervals exclude zero and the two whose intervals do not are not two different kinds of result. They sit on a continuum of evidence. Drawing a line at 0.05 and sorting them into “real” and “not real” throws away the fact that FGRAZE2:LOGAREA (p = 0.08) and FGRAZE3:LOGAREA (p = 0.005) differ in degree, not in kind.

E4. linear_model_4: new Q12 (after Q11)

12. Final write-up, and the least guidance yet. You have arrived at a model by a selection procedure, which raises reporting problems that the previous three exercises did not.

Write no more than 200 words describing this analysis for the methods and results of a paper. Your reader is an ecologist who is competent but not a statistician, and who was not in the room while you did the analysis. They must be able to tell from your text exactly what you did and how much to trust it.

Think about what an honest account has to include that a naive one would leave out. Then, in a sentence or two each:

  1. You started with 14 parameters and ended with 10. If you reported only the final model and said nothing about how you got there, what would a reader be unable to judge?

  2. Your maximal model produced 14 p values. If none of the explanatory variables had any real effect, roughly how many would you still expect to fall below 0.05? What does that imply about interpreting a large table of tests?

  3. The p values and confidence intervals in your final model are calculated as if you had specified that model in advance. You did not; you arrived at it by looking at these same data. Does that make them too optimistic, too conservative, or unaffected? You are not expected to know the machinery for fixing this, only to recognise that it is there.

Model answer for the solutions file

(preceded by the standing caveat)

Methods. Bird abundance was modelled against log10 patch area, log10 distance to the nearest patch, log10 distance to the nearest larger patch, year of isolation, altitude and grazing intensity (a five-level factor), plus the area by grazing interaction. Distance and area variables were log10 transformed after graphical exploration indicated strong right skew. Terms were removed by backward selection using single-term deletions and F tests, with main effects retained wherever they appeared in a retained interaction.

Results. Four terms were removed in turn (distance to nearest larger patch, year of isolation, distance to nearest patch, altitude), leaving patch area, grazing intensity and their interaction. Bird abundance increased with patch area at every grazing level, but at a rate that varied roughly fourfold with grazing intensity (interaction F4,57 = 4.09, p = 0.006; R² = 0.80, n = 67). Because the final model was selected using these data, the quoted p values and intervals are optimistic and should be treated as descriptive.

(≈190 words)

  1. They could not judge how much searching was done. A model reported without its selection history looks as though it was specified in advance on theoretical grounds, which carries far more evidential weight than a model arrived at by trying and discarding alternatives. A reader also cannot tell which variables were measured and found unhelpful, which is genuinely useful information for anyone designing a follow-up study. Report the starting model, the criterion, and the order of removal.

  2. At a 0.05 threshold you expect about 1 in 20 tests to fall below it by chance alone, so with 14 tests roughly 0.7, call it one, even if nothing is going on. This is why a single starred coefficient in a large table is weak evidence, and why hunting through a big model for whatever happens to be below 0.05 is a poor way to do science. It is also why the selection procedure here is driven by a stated rule applied consistently rather than by picking out whatever looks interesting.

  3. Too optimistic. The selection procedure kept precisely those terms that looked strongest in this particular sample, so their estimates are biased away from zero and their intervals are too narrow. The problem is known as post-selection inference and there are methods for handling it (sample splitting, bootstrapping the whole selection procedure, or fitting the pre-specified model of linear_model_3 and reporting that instead). You are not expected to apply them here. You are expected to know that the caveat exists and to say so when you report a selected model, because the great majority of published papers that use stepwise selection do not.

Worth noticing: the model you arrived at by selection is exactly the model you fitted by hand in linear_model_3, where it was specified in advance from the study’s own hypotheses. That is a genuinely reassuring outcome and it is worth saying so in a write-up. It also illustrates the point in (c): the version of this model that was specified in advance carries more weight than the version that was selected, even though the numbers are identical.
